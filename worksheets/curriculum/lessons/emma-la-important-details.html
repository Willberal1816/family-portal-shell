Find the Important Details

Emma · Language Arts · Learn First
What you are learning: Before writing a summary, decide which details are necessary to understand the main event or idea and which details can be left out.

A summary is smaller than the original

A good summary keeps the most important information but removes extra description, repeated facts, and small side details. It should tell a reader what happened or what the passage mainly explains without retelling every sentence.

  • main idea or main event: the most important point the passage is about
  • important detail: a fact a reader needs to understand that main point
  • extra detail: a fact that may be interesting but is not necessary for the summary

Worked example

Passage: Maya wanted to grow tomatoes on her apartment balcony. She chose a sunny corner, filled a large pot with soil, and planted a young tomato plant. Every morning she checked the soil and watered when it felt dry. Her blue watering can had a yellow handle. After several weeks, the plant produced its first red tomato.

Main event: Maya successfully grew a tomato plant on her balcony.

Important details: She chose a sunny place, cared for the plant by checking and watering it, and the plant eventually produced a tomato.

Extra detail: The watering can had a yellow handle. That color does not help explain how the plant grew.

Use the “Does the reader need this?” test

For each detail, ask: If I remove this, can a reader still understand the main event or idea? If yes, it may be extra. If removing it makes the main idea confusing or incomplete, it is probably important.

Worked decision: “The balcony was sunny” matters because sunlight helped the tomato plant grow. “The watering can had a yellow handle” does not explain the growth, so it can be left out.

Do not replace details with opinions

A summary should stay faithful to the passage. Do not add what you think the character should have done or facts that were not stated.
