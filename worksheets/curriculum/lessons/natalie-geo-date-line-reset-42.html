Date Line: Which Way Changes the Date?

Today's objective: Apply correct westbound/eastbound date changes on a simplified model and explain why real time zones and political boundaries matter.

Approved background reading: Open this professional explanation if helpful. Everything needed for today's work is taught below and printed on the sheets.

Learn First: read each step

  1. The International Date Line (IDL) runs mostly through the Pacific near 180° longitude. In this simplified model, the WEST side (parts of Asia/Pacific) is one calendar day ahead of the EAST side (parts of the Americas).
  2. When you CROSS the IDL WESTWARD (east side → west side), move the calendar one day FORWARD. When you CROSS EASTWARD (west side → east side), move it one day BACKWARD.
  3. The date changes, but a real trip still takes time and each region sets its civil time-zone rules. The simplified same-clock examples below isolate DATE change only; they are NOT a real flight timetable.
  4. For any answer, say direction, starting side, and the new DAY and DATE. Do not confuse east-later UTC clock reasoning with the date-line crossing rule.

See the two fully worked examples

Worked example 1: Fictional westbound crossing from the EAST side of the IDL on Sunday, November 8 → WEST side is Monday, November 9 on our simplified same-clock model. We ADD one calendar day.
Worked example 2: Fictional eastbound crossing from the WEST side on Monday, November 9 → EAST side is Sunday, November 8 on our simplified model. We SUBTRACT one calendar day. Real legal time zones and flight durations may differ.
Watch for this common error: Do not reverse the directions: westbound gains a DATE, eastbound loses a DATE. These are calendar-label comparisons across the IDL, not extra 24 hours physically spent traveling.
