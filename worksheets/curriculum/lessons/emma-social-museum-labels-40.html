Museum Labels: What the Record Really Says

Emma · Social Studies · Thursday, October 29, 2026 · 45 minutes

What you will be able to do: Compare a dated record and a museum display, distinguish factual evidence from an interpretation, and revise an overconfident claim.

Learn First — follow these three steps

  1. A primary record comes from the time being studied; a display label written later summarizes an interpretation. Both can be useful but answer different questions.
  2. A date and a place define the scope of a source. A single record cannot automatically describe what happened in every place or year.
  3. A good comparison identifies what each source actually says, what they agree on, and what is still unknown.

See the exact worked examples

Worked example A: Source A, a 1910 receipt, shows three chairs sold in one shop. You may say 'Three chairs appear on this shop's 1910 receipt,' but you cannot say 'All shops sold three chairs in 1910.'
Worked example B: A modern label describes what museum staff believe about past routines. If the original log mentions only a work shift, the label's claim about why a shift changed needs a separate source.
Check this common mistake: Common mistake: reading a museum explanation as if an older record proves every phrase on the sign. Keep fact and interpretation separate.
