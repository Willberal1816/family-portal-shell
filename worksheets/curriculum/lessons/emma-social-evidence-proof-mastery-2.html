What Can the Evidence Really Prove?

Goal: Build a conclusion from named evidence without adding details the sources do not prove. Then choose a genuinely new source and explain exactly what it could add.

Today’s path

  1. Learn one simple evidence rule.
  2. Watch two worked examples.
  3. Do one guided example with the answer steps shown.
  4. Complete a fresh independent check.

The evidence rule

Fact → Source → What it proves → What it does NOT prove.

Before writing a conclusion, name the source and ask: “Can I point to the exact words, number, time, picture, or record that proves this?” If not, leave that detail out.

Worked example 1 — library closing

Source A — library notice: “Building closed at 4:30 p.m. because the fire alarm activated.”
Source B — security log: “4:28 p.m. alarm sounded; 4:31 p.m. front doors locked.”

What both support: the library closed around 4:30 after the alarm.

What B adds: exact times for the alarm and locked doors.

What neither proves: what caused the alarm.

Safe conclusion: “The alarm sounded at 4:28, and the building was closed around 4:30. The sources do not say what caused the alarm.”

Worked example 2 — bus delay

Source C — bus GPS log: “Bus 14 reached Oak Street at 8:17 a.m.”
Source D — school attendance note: “Bus 14 students were admitted late at 8:24 a.m.”

Supported: Bus 14 and its students were late that morning.

Not supported: “The bus was late because of traffic.” No source gives the cause.

A genuinely new source: a traffic-camera record from Oak Street. What it could add: whether congestion blocked the bus route at that time.

Before you write any conclusion

  1. Underline the exact evidence you will use.
  2. Name the source beside each fact.
  3. Cross out any idea you cannot prove from a source.
  4. If the question asks for a new source, name the actual record/person/object and say the exact missing fact it could add.
