Data Detective: Which Day Was Typical?

Emma · Math · Saturday, November 14, 2026 · 50 minutes

What you are learning

Use a five-day data table to calculate a total, mean and range, then say exactly what those numbers can and cannot prove.

Learn First — Teaching

  1. Look at the five recorded days separately. A number in a data table is evidence for that day, not a prediction for every future day.
  2. A TOTAL counts all observations. Add the five daily values carefully and check by adding them in another order.
  3. A MEAN is a typical-number summary: add the data, then divide by how many values you counted. Here each reading room has FIVE school-day counts.
  4. A RANGE tells how spread out the data are. Subtract the lowest count from the highest count; the range is a number of checkouts, not a number of days.
  5. Two rooms can have the same mean but different daily patterns. Compare one number at a time before you draw a conclusion.
  6. A conclusion should name the specific recorded week. It should not say one library is always busier based on only five days.
  7. To check a mean, multiply the answer by five; it should equal your total. Label all final answers with units such as books.

Two fully worked models

Model 1

Worked model: Room A checkouts are 8, 12, 10, 14, 6. Total = 50 books. Mean = 50 divided by 5 = 10 books per day. Check: 10 times 5 = 50.

Model 2

Worked model: Room A's highest count is 14 and lowest 6; its range is 14 minus 6 = 8 checkouts. This describes variation, NOT how long the library opened.

Important mistake to avoid:

Do not divide by the number of different counts. Divide by the number of DAYS, which is five. Do not confuse a mean with the biggest value.
