Batch Lab: Scale a Recipe, Prove the Measure

Natalie | Cooking/Life Skills | Friday, November 13, 2026 | 50 minutes

What you are learning

Scale a water-only recipe by the serving factor, physically measure a test batch, audit a wrong scaling method and document actual kitchen cleanup.

Learn First: follow the reasoning

  1. This is a safe no-heat simulation of a recipe. Water is the ONLY ingredient to measure; the quantities are mathematical practice, not drink or food instructions.
  2. A sample recipe uses THREE QUARTERS of a cup of water PER serving. Scaling means multiplying amount per serving by number of servings.
  3. For four servings, multiply four by three quarters of a cup: twelve quarters of a cup, or THREE full cups.
  4. For six servings, multiply six by three quarters: eighteen quarters of a cup, or FOUR AND A HALF full cups.
  5. To MEASURE four and a half cups in a safe bowl, use four level one-cup pours plus one level half-cup pour; alternatively plan an equivalent accurate sequence.
  6. Before measuring, wash hands, level the measuring cups, check a stable tray and clean bowl, then record every actual pour. If the bowl is small, split into safely measurable batches and RECORD the split.
  7. Error audit: four servings requiring three cups cannot become four cups just because the serving count is four. A per-serving fraction must be multiplied, not replaced by one cup.
  8. Close the lab: empty/reuse the water, dry counter safely, return tools, and record genuine setup/measurement/cleanup times. Do not invent observations.

Two fully worked models

Model 1

Model A: Recipe: three quarters cup per serving. Four servings = four groups of three quarters = twelve quarters = three cups. Verify: three cups divided equally across four servings is three quarters of a cup each.

Model 2

Model B: Six servings = eighteen quarter-cup units = four full cups plus two quarter-cups, which equals four and a half cups.

A mistake to avoid

Mixing up number of servings and number of cups changes the recipe. Use the per-serving quantity every time, and distinguish planned from actually measured water.
