Cross Zero, Then Check the Date

Natalie · Geography · Monday, September 28, 2026 · Learn First

What you are learning: calculate a difference across zero longitude in the correct direction, and separate a solar-time estimate from a real legal time-zone rule. Read the examples, then use the separate Independent Work printable for all answers. This teaching page has no written work to turn in.

Learn the idea

Longitude values tell east-west position. For a simplified solar-time estimate, divide the longitude difference by 15 degrees per hour. From a western longitude to an eastern longitude across the prime meridian, add the two magnitudes. From 75°W to 45°E: (75+45)÷15=8 hours later eastward. From 30°E to 60°W the difference is (30+60)÷15=6 hours earlier westward.

Real civil time zones can bend around borders; this classroom 15° rule is an estimate, not a promise of the clock time in an actual named city. The International Date Line changes the calendar date: moving west across it advances a date; moving east subtracts a date. Keep the clock and date as separate checks.

Words that help

  • UTC offset: hours added to or subtracted from UTC for this classroom card
  • midnight rollover: passing 24:00 or going below 00:00 changes date
  • International Date Line: a convention where crossing direction changes calendar date

Worked example 1

90°W → 30°E crosses zero. Difference 90+30=120°, so 120÷15=8 hours. Traveling east means later solar time. If the first clock is 8 AM, estimate 4 PM the same day.

Worked example 2

60°E → 15°W crosses zero traveling west. Difference 60+15=75°, so 75÷15=5 hours earlier. If the first is 2 PM, the estimate is 9 AM. Do not subtract 60−15 here.

Move EAST → later local solar time. Move WEST → earlier. Difference in degrees ÷15 ≈ hours. Across 0°, ADD both magnitudes. Across the International Date Line: west adds a date, east subtracts a date (clock offsets also matter).
