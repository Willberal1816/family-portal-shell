Science: A Fair Friction Comparison

Emma | Science | Wednesday, November 25, 2026 | 50 minutes

What you are learning: Use recorded distances, mean, fair-test controls and cautious evidence claims.

Learn First

Friction acts against motion when surfaces touch. In a fair comparison change only the surface. Use the same toy car, ramp, starting point, release and method for measuring each distance. If you change the car and surface together, you cannot tell which factor changed the result.

Mean distance = sum of trial distances divided by the number of trials. Label the result in inches. Check by multiplying the mean by trial count to see whether you get the sum. A bar chart starts at zero and compares the two means; title the vertical axis “Mean distance (inches)”.

Limit the conclusion to the observed trials. Distance is not speed unless you also measured time. Name what you would measure and keep the release area clear in a real test. Today you analyze the fictional data, so do not report a real experiment.

Two worked examples

Practice test: three distances of 10, 12 and 14 inches. Sum = 36 in; mean = 36 / 3 = 12 in. Reverse check 12 x 3 = 36.
Practice comparison: one mean is 15 inches and the other is 9 inches. The difference is 6 inches in these trials. This does not prove every car behaves that way or that one car was faster.

Source and materials

FICTIONAL TOY-CAR TEST; DISTANCES IN INCHES
Same car, same ramp and release position.
Cardboard: 28, 30, 32 inches.
Low-pile carpet: 16, 18, 20 inches.
Three trials per surface; no speed measurements.

All six questions

Question 1. Name the one changed factor, the outcome measured, and three factors kept constant. Explain why also changing the toy car would make the test unfair.
Question 2. Create a six-row data table with surface, trial number and travel distance in inches.
Question 3. Find each surface's mean distance, showing the sum and division. Find the difference, and check one mean by multiplication. Label all units.
Question 4. Draw a two-bar chart of the two means using the grid. Start the vertical scale at zero and label the units.
Question 5. Write one conclusion supported by these trials and one claim about all cars or speed that the data cannot prove. Explain what extra data would be needed.
Question 6. Plan a safe four-step repeat: name what changes, three controls, the measurement procedure and how you will decide whether the result supports your claim.
