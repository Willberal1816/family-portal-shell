Harbor Records: Fact, Sequence, or Cause?

Learn the idea

A historical source has a date, a creator, and a job. A log records selected events. A letter records one person’s experience. A later brochure retells the past from much later. Before you make a claim, identify what each source can actually prove.

Sequence is not the same as cause. If fog appeared before a ferry stopped, the order is real, but the order alone does not prove fog caused the stop. To claim a cause, look for evidence that connects the proposed cause to the effect. If the record names a broken signal rope as the reason service stopped, that is stronger causal evidence than the fact that fog also came first.

The four-step proof ladder

StepQuestion to askGood move
1. LabelWho made this source, and when?Write “Harbor Board log, May 3, 1912.”
2. CopyWhat exact fact does it record?Keep the date, number, and careful verb.
3. OrderWhat happened before and after?Build a dated sequence without adding events.
4. TestDoes the source connect a cause to an effect?Say “supported,” “possible,” or “not shown,” then explain why.

Fictional harbor source packet

Source A — Harbor Board log, May 3, 1912

“7:00 — heavy fog over the east channel. 7:20 — east signal-bell rope found broken. 7:35 — ferry service stopped because the east signal could not be sounded. 8:10 — mechanic arrived. 9:05 — rope replaced. 9:20 — ferry service resumed.”

Source B — Letter from bakery worker Lena Ortiz, May 3, 1912

“I waited at the bakery until after nine because the ferry had stopped. The fog was thick, and several customers arrived late.”

Source C — Harbor anniversary brochure, 1985

“The great fog of 1912 shut the harbor all morning and changed every business forever.”

Worked example 1 — exact fact or changed fact?

Weak: “The harbor closed at seven because of fog.” That changes ferry service into the whole harbor, changes the stop time, and invents a cause.

Strong: “Source A records that ferry service stopped at 7:35 because the east signal could not be sounded.” The sentence keeps the source’s time, event, and stated reason.

Worked example 2 — sequence or cause?

Sequence: fog at 7:00 → broken rope found at 7:20 → ferry stopped at 7:35 → rope replaced at 9:05 → ferry resumed at 9:20.

Cause claim: Source A directly connects the missing signal to the service stop. The sources show fog was present, but none says fog caused the rope to break. Therefore, “fog broke the rope” is not supported.

Common mistakes to avoid

MistakeRepair
Using a later brochure as if it were an event-day recordLabel it as a later retelling and check its claims against earlier sources.
Turning one person’s experience into “everyone”Write “Lena Ortiz reported…” and keep the claim limited to her letter.
Writing “A happened first, so A caused B”Name the evidence that connects A to B, or say the cause is not proven.
Changing careful source wordsCopy exact dates, numbers, and verbs before explaining.
