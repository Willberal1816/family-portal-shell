Kinds of Sentences

Natalie · Language Arts · Learn First
Goal: Learn how simple, compound, and complex sentences are built. You are not just memorizing labels—you are learning how writers control ideas.

1. Start with clauses

A clause is a group of words with a subject and a verb. Some clauses can stand alone; some cannot.

Independent clause
A complete thought that can be a sentence.
The dog barked.
Dependent clause
Has a subject and verb but leaves the reader waiting for more.
Because the dog barked...

Words such as because, although, when, while, if, since often turn a complete thought into a dependent clause.

2. Three useful sentence patterns

Simple: one independent clause. It can still be long.
The tired dog near the porch barked loudly at the delivery truck.
Compound: two independent clauses joined correctly.
The dog barked, but the baby stayed asleep.
Common joining words: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so.
Complex: one independent clause plus at least one dependent clause.
Because the dog barked, I looked outside.

3. Watch for two common mistakes

Fragment: “Because the rain started.” This leaves us waiting. Fix it: “Because the rain started, we went inside.”
Comma splice: “The rain stopped, we went outside.” Two complete thoughts cannot be joined by only a comma. Fix it with a period, semicolon, or a comma + joining word.

4. Work through these with the explanation

A. “Although I was tired, I finished the chapter.” The first part cannot stand alone, but the second can. That makes it complex.

B. “I finished the chapter, and I wrote three notes.” Both halves can stand alone. That makes it compound.

C. “My sister and I read on the couch.” It has two subjects, but only one complete thought. It is still simple.

5. Check yourself before practice

Teaching example: “When the timer rang, we checked the oven.” What kind?
Answer / reasoning: Complex. “When the timer rang” depends on the rest of the sentence.
Teaching example: “The cookies cooled, and we washed the dishes.” What kind?
Answer / reasoning: Compound. Both sides can stand alone, joined with and.
Teaching example: “The warm cookies smelled amazing.” What kind?
Answer / reasoning: Simple. One independent clause.
Ready for practice when: you can explain the difference between an independent and dependent clause without looking back.
