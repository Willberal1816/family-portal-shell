What Changed at the Harbor?

Natalie · Social Studies · 20 minutes Learn First + 30 minutes independent history work

What you are learning: Historians compare records to explain a change. Use the date, creator, purpose, direct observation, and missing information to decide what a source can establish. A timeline shows order; evidence is required to claim a cause. This is a fictional classroom case, not a claim about an actual town.

1. A short reminder from your map lesson

A map made in one year cannot by itself prove how far someone traveled years earlier. Always ask: Who made this? When? For what purpose? What does it leave out? A map scale can estimate distance, but not travel time or motives without more records.

2. Riverton's bridge, May 1912 (fictional source set)

DateRecord
May 3Heavy rainfall recorded by the station.
May 4Bridge inspector notes a damaged support; recommends closing the bridge until checked.
May 5Town posts a bridge closure notice.
May 8Temporary ferry service begins.
May 11A shopkeeper describes later deliveries.
May 20Repair work begins.
Source A — inspector's note, May 4: “The north support has a visible split. Do not allow wagons across until the support is inspected and repaired.” Creator/purpose: bridge inspector reporting a safety finding.
Source B — town notice, May 5: “Bridge closed to all wagons while the north support is inspected. Use the temporary riverside route.” Creator/purpose: town office informing travelers.
Source C — shopkeeper's letter, May 11: “Our flour arrived two days later than usual this week. I used the ferry but did not see the bridge support myself.” Creator/purpose: shopkeeper describing the delivery experience.
Source D — museum caption, written in 1940: “A storm destroyed the bridge and stopped all trade.” Creator/purpose: later summary for visitors; it does not cite its supporting records.

3. Model: claim, exact evidence, limit

Careful supported claim: The bridge was closed after an inspector reported a visible split in its support. Evidence: Source A describes the support and advises no wagons; Source B announces the closure the following day. Limit: Neither record proves that rainfall alone created the split.
A second careful claim: At least one merchant experienced a delivery delay. Source C reports a specific two-day delay. It does not show that every merchant had a delay or that trade stopped entirely.

4. How historians test a possible cause

  1. Establish the order. Rain was recorded before the inspection and closure.
  2. Find direct evidence of the decision. The inspector's safety note and closure notice directly connect the damaged support to the closure.
  3. Test the proposed cause. Rain could have contributed, but the records do not establish the support's previous condition or rule out another cause.
  4. Check competing statements. The museum caption says the bridge was destroyed and all trade stopped. The note says visible split, and the ferry operated, so that wording exceeds the evidence.

5. Practice together

Question: Does Source C prove the bridge was unsafe? Answer: No. The shopkeeper directly witnessed a delayed delivery but did not inspect the support. Source A is the direct source for the support's condition.
