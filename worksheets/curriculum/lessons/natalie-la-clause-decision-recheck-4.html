Clause Decision Recheck

What you are learning

A clause is a group of words with its own subject and verb. Your job is to decide whether each whole clause can stand alone as a complete thought. After that, the sentence type comes from the I/D pattern.

Use the same decision ladder every time

1. Find each whole clause. Look for each subject-and-verb group.

2. Read one clause by itself. If it gives a complete thought, mark I for independent. If it leaves you waiting for the rest of the idea, mark D for dependent.

3. Name the pattern. I + I = compound. D + I or I + D = complex. At least two I clauses plus at least one D clause = compound-complex.

Worked example 1: one dependent clause

Because the sidewalk was wet, I wore my old shoes.

Because the sidewalk was wet has a subject (sidewalk) and verb (was), but “Because…” makes you wait to hear what happened. That whole clause is D.

I wore my old shoes makes a complete thought by itself. It is I.

Pattern: D + I → complex sentence.

Worked example 2: two complete thoughts

The timer rang, so we took the bread out.

The timer rang can stand alone → I.

we took the bread out can stand alone → I.

Pattern: I + I → compound sentence. The comma comes before so because it joins two independent clauses.

Worked example 3: three clauses

When the timer rang, I checked the bread, and Dad opened the oven.

When the timer rang leaves you waiting for what happened next → D.

I checked the bread can stand alone → I.

Dad opened the oven can stand alone → I.

Pattern: D + I + I → compound-complex sentence.

Watch for this common mistake

A word such as because, when, although, after, while, until, or if can turn a subject-and-verb group into a dependent clause.

Example: the rain stopped is a complete thought, but until the rain stopped leaves you waiting for the rest of the idea. The second one is D.

Do not name the sentence type from a joining word alone. Find every whole clause, test each one, then use the I/D pattern.

What the independent recheck will ask you to prove

You will classify fresh sentences, correct an incorrect clause decision, compare two similar sentence patterns, build one sentence of your own, and explain the stand-alone test. The goal is to show that you can use the decision ladder on new examples without guessing from a single word.
