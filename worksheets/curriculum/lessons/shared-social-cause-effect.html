Cause, Effect & Chronology

What this lesson teaches: put events in time order and distinguish “happened before” from evidence that one event actually contributed to another. All required ordering and causal reasoning is on the printable.

Definitions

Chronology: the order in which events happened.

Cause: a factor that helps produce an event or change.

Effect: a result that follows from one or more causes.

Evidence of causation: information showing a meaningful connection, not just that one thing occurred first.

Worked chronology

Heavy rain falls
→
River rises
→
Road closes

Cleanup begins later, after water recedes. Chronology helps organize the sequence before deciding which relationships are causal.

Immediate versus longer-term effects

A flood can immediately close roads and damage buildings. A longer-term effect might be repairs, changed insurance costs, or a new flood-control project. One event can have several effects on different timescales.

Earlier does not automatically mean cause

A school bell rings before rain starts. The bell did not cause the rain. To argue causation, evidence must connect the proposed cause to the effect in a plausible way.

Testing a cause claim

If someone claims heavy rain caused a road closure, useful evidence could include rainfall records, river-level measurements, closure notices, photographs, and reports explaining why officials closed the road.
