Different Points of View

Emma + Natalie · Social Studies · Learn First
What you are learning: Compare how people can describe the same event differently because of position, experience, purpose, timing, and access to information.

Learn the idea

Two sources can disagree without one person automatically lying. People notice different details because they stand in different places, have different jobs, know different information, or care about different outcomes. Historians call this perspective.

Perspective is not an excuse to accept every claim. Compare each source with other evidence. Ask who created it, when, what the person could directly observe, what they might have missed, and why they recorded the event.

Agreement between independent sources can strengthen a claim, while disagreement can reveal what needs more investigation. A merchant may emphasize damaged goods; a city official may emphasize road closures; a child may remember frightening sounds. Different views can describe different parts of the same event.

Purpose matters. A private diary, advertisement, official report, and campaign speech target different audiences. Purpose does not automatically make a source unreliable, but it helps explain emphasis and omissions.

Words you will use

  • perspective: viewpoint shaped by position, experience, and knowledge
  • purpose: why a source was created
  • audience: who the source was meant for
  • corroborate: compare independent evidence
  • limitation: what a source cannot reliably tell us

Worked examples

Example: a worker inside a fire may describe smoke and evacuation while a reporter outside describes fire crews and roads.
Purpose: an advertisement may accurately name a product but exaggerate benefits.
Comparison: three independent sources agreeing a bridge closed at noon strengthen that detail.

More worked examples

Teaching example: Does a different perspective automatically make a source false?
Answer / reasoning: No.
Teaching example: What identifies a source limitation?
Answer / reasoning: Ask what the creator could directly know and what was outside their access.
Teaching example: Why does audience matter?
Answer / reasoning: Writers select tone/details based on whom they want to inform or persuade.
