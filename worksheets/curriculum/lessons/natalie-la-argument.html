Claim, Evidence & Reasoning

Natalie · Language Arts · Guided Teaching
What you are learning: Separate an author’s claim from the evidence used to support it, then judge whether the reasoning actually connects the evidence to the claim.

Three different jobs

  • Claim: what the author wants the reader to accept.
  • Evidence: facts, examples, observations, or data offered in support.
  • Reasoning: the explanation of why that evidence makes the claim more believable.
Example: Claim: the town should add a crosswalk. Evidence: traffic counts show 420 cars pass during the school arrival hour. Reasoning: heavy traffic increases the difficulty of crossing safely, so a marked crossing could reduce risk.

Evidence must fit the claim

A true fact is not automatically useful evidence. Ask whether the fact actually bears on the point the author is trying to prove.

Weak evidence: “The school mascot is a hawk” does not support a claim about traffic safety, even though it may be true.

Check the reasoning

Good reasoning does not simply repeat the claim. It shows the bridge between evidence and conclusion. Watch for missing steps, exaggeration, and evidence that supports only part of a claim.
