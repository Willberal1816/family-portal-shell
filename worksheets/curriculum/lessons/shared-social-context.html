Source Context & Reliability

What this lesson teaches: judge how useful a source is for a particular question by considering who created it, when, why, and what access the creator had. Required analysis is on the printable worksheet.

Definitions

Context: the circumstances around a source—creator, time, place, purpose, and audience.

Reliability: how dependable a source is for the specific claim being investigated.

Bias/perspective: a viewpoint that can shape what a source notices, emphasizes, or leaves out. Bias does not automatically make a source useless.

Four context checks

CheckWhat it can reveal
Who?Experience, role, viewpoint, access.
When?How close the source is to the event and whether memory or later information may affect it.
Why?Purpose—inform, persuade, advertise, record, remember, defend, etc.
Access?What the creator could actually see, measure, know, or verify.

Worked example — next-day newspaper

A newspaper report written the next morning may be useful because it is close in time and may combine several interviews. It may still contain incomplete early information because the investigation is not finished.

Worked example — memoir decades later

A memoir written 40 years later may add personal reflection and details about how the event felt. Memory can change or blend details over time, so exact dates or quotations may need corroboration.

Worked example — official report

An official report may be strong evidence for recorded totals, dates, or procedures. It may be weaker for understanding private feelings or experiences that were never recorded.

Why a biased source can still matter

A campaign poster is designed to persuade, so it is poor evidence for a neutral account of every fact. But it is excellent evidence for what the campaign wanted people to believe and which messages it emphasized.
