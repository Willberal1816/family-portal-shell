Trail Cleanup: Count the Respondents, Limit the Claim

Natalie | Language Arts | Friday, November 13, 2026 | 55 minutes

What you are learning

Build a supported multi-source account distinguishing signups, actual work, one volunteer's opinion and unproved causes.

Learn First: follow the reasoning

  1. Each source answers a different question. A sign-up sheet counts people who SIGNED UP; it does not prove every sign-up attended.
  2. A work log can report the number of workers checked in on a particular day. It may not capture people who never checked in.
  3. A personal quotation is one person's opinion and cannot establish what all volunteers believed.
  4. Good reporting names source, date, place and population. 'Nine of fourteen Saturday signups checked in' is narrower than 'everyone came.'
  5. Sequence tells us two things happened in order; it does not establish that the first CAUSED the second. To claim a cause, you need evidence connecting the events.
  6. A strong paragraph presents the organizer's plan, the exact counts, the one person's comment, a comparison and the missing information. Put numbers in distinct sentences.
  7. When an overclaim has two faults, correct BOTH. For example, 'All visitors loved the new trail because of the flyer' lacks population support and a cause link.

Two fully worked models

Model 1

Model A: Fourteen signed up on Tuesday, nine checked in Saturday. The log supports 'Nine checked in Saturday'; it does not establish why five names did not check in.

Model 2

Model B: A quote by one volunteer says the trail is useful. You may say 'One volunteer called the trail useful,' not 'Every resident agrees the cleanup made the trail useful.'

A mistake to avoid

Do not silently turn sign-ups into attendance, and never present one testimony as a community-wide vote.
