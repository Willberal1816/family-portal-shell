Add Strong Details

Emma · Language Arts · Learn First
What you are learning: Choose specific details that help a reader picture, understand, or believe the main idea of a paragraph.

Learn the idea

A detail is useful when it does a job. It can help the reader picture a scene, understand what happened, or see why a main idea is true. Adding random adjectives does not automatically make writing stronger.

Compare “The dog was funny” with “The dog carried one sock to the door and waited as if it were a leash.” The second sentence gives a concrete action so the reader can see why the dog seems funny.

Strong details often use specific nouns and verbs. “The bird moved” can become “The robin hopped along the fence.” You do not need giant vocabulary words; you need accurate ones.

Good writers remove details that pull away from the main idea. If a paragraph is about why a storm canceled a picnic, the blanket color probably does not matter unless it connects to the point.

Words you will use

  • specific: exact enough to create a clear idea
  • detail: information developing a main idea
  • relevant: connected to the paragraph’s purpose
  • revise: change writing to make ideas stronger or clearer

Worked examples

Vague: “The room was messy.” Specific: “Shoes blocked the doorway and open boxes covered the floor.”
Weak verb: “The squirrel went across the branch.” Stronger: “The squirrel scrambled across the branch.”
Relevant detail: for a difficult hike, steep hills and loose rocks matter more than a backpack color.

More worked examples

Teaching example: Which is stronger: “The soup was hot” or “Steam fogged my glasses”?
Answer / reasoning: The second gives an observable detail.
Teaching example: Should every sentence have many adjectives?
Answer / reasoning: No; precise nouns, verbs, and facts are more useful.
Teaching example: What revision question helps test a detail?
Answer / reasoning: How does this help the reader understand my main idea?
