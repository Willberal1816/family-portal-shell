Story Clues — Make the Evidence Fit

This lesson reteaches the same prerequisite with a clearer evidence-to-inference structure. The focus is choosing the strongest clue and explaining exactly how that clue supports the idea.

2. Definitions and the evidence ladder

Inference: an idea the reader figures out from clues.

Text evidence: the exact action, detail, or words in the passage that support an idea.

Strongest clue: the detail that most directly supports the inference and requires the least guessing.

1. Idea
What is probably true?
2. Exact clue
Which words or actions show it?
3. Connection
Why does that clue support the idea?

3. Worked example — strongest clue

Text: “Maya checked the clock twice, kept her backpack by the door, and hummed while she waited.”

Inference: Maya is eager to leave.

Strongest clue: “kept her backpack by the door,” because that action directly shows she is ready to go. Humming could mean many things, so it is weaker evidence.

4. Worked example — make the idea fit the clues

Clues: Leo reread the directions, tightened one loose piece, and carried the model with both hands.

Strong inference: The model matters to Leo and he wants to protect it.

Weak inference: Leo is angry. Nothing in the clues shows anger.

5. More worked examples

Example: Ava puts on her shoes, stands beside the door, and looks out the window every minute. A supported inference is that she is eager or impatient to leave; the repeated looking and waiting by the door directly support it.

Example: Ben folds the paper carefully, erases one crooked line, and starts again. Starting again is especially strong evidence that he cares about doing the work well.

Example: Sara whispers, keeps looking toward the hallway, and hides a gift bag. “Sara is bored” is weak because the clues fit secrecy or anticipation much better.

Example clue for “the character is relieved”: “He let out a long breath and smiled when the missing dog ran into the yard.”

6. Common mistakes and corrections

Weak response: “The strongest clue is that Nina smiled because smiles are in stories.”

Why it is weak: it does not name the inference and does not explain what the smile shows.

Stronger model: “The strongest clue is that Nina smiled when she saw the test score because that reaction supports the inference that she was pleased with the result.”
