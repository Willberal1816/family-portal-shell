Story Clues — Make the Evidence Fit

This is a new class on the same prerequisite. Your answers often had a reasonable idea; the part that needs to become automatic is choosing the strongest clue and making the explanation match that exact clue.

Plan for about 55 minutes

  1. Video teaching: about 8 minutes.
  2. Clue-strength model: about 10 minutes.
  3. Worked examples: about 10 minutes.
  4. Guided practice: about 12 minutes.
  5. Mistake detective: about 5 minutes.
  6. Independent Work: about 10 minutes.

1. Watch a different explanation

As you watch, notice that an inference is built from text evidence—not from a guess that could fit any story.

Video not playing? Open the source.

2. Use the three-step evidence ladder

1. Idea
What do I think is true?
2. Exact clue
Which words/actions show it?
3. Connection
Why does that clue support the idea?

If one step does not match the others, the answer is weak even if one part sounds good.

3. Worked example — strongest clue

Text: “Maya checked the clock twice, kept her backpack by the door, and hummed while she waited.”

Inference: Maya is eager to leave.

The strongest clue is “kept her backpack by the door” because that action shows she is ready to go. Humming could mean many things, so it is weaker evidence.

4. Worked example — make the idea fit the clues

Clues: Leo reread the directions, tightened one loose piece, and carried the model with both hands.

A strong inference is: The model matters to Leo and he wants to protect it.

A weak inference would be: Leo is angry. Nothing in the clues actually shows anger.

5. Guided practice — answer before checking

  1. Text: Ava put on her shoes before anyone asked, stood beside the door, and looked out the window every minute. What can you infer?
    CheckShe is eager or impatient to leave. The repeated looking and waiting by the door directly support it.
  2. Text: Ben folded the paper carefully, erased one crooked line, and started again. Which clue best shows he cares about doing the work well?
    CheckStarting again after noticing a crooked line is especially strong because it shows he is willing to redo work for quality.
  3. Clues: Sara whispers, keeps looking toward the hallway, and hides the gift bag behind her chair. Is “Sara is bored” a good inference? Explain.
    CheckNo. The clues fit secrecy or anticipation much better than boredom.
  4. Write one clue that would strongly support the inference “The character is relieved.”
    Example after you write yours“He let out a long breath and smiled when the missing dog ran into the yard.”

6. Mistake detective

Student answer: “The strongest clue is that Nina smiled because smiles are in stories.”

Identify two problems with the explanation, then rewrite it so the clue connects to a specific inference.

Check

The response does not name the inference and does not explain what the smile shows. A better answer might be: “The strongest clue is that Nina smiled when she saw the test score because that reaction supports the inference that she was pleased with the result.”

7. Independent Work

Print and complete the attached Independent Work page. It is the independent evidence for today; there is no extra mini-test afterward.

  • Answer every numbered part.
  • Name or copy the exact clue when asked.
  • If asked for the strongest clue, compare it with the weaker details.
  • Make sure the inference and the clue point to the same idea.
