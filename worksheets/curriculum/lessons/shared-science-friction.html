Friction Test

Emma + Natalie · Science · Learn First
What you are learning: Explain friction as a contact force, design a fair surface comparison, collect repeated measurements, and use evidence to support a claim.

Learn the idea

Friction is a contact force that resists sliding between surfaces. It acts opposite the direction surfaces move or try to move relative to each other. Friction can slow motion, prevent slipping, create grip, and produce heat.

How much friction occurs depends on the surfaces and how strongly they press together. In a classroom test, surface material is easier to investigate. Keep the object, starting position, travel distance, and release or pull method the same.

You can compare friction without expensive equipment. Slide the same object down the same-height ramp covered with different materials and measure how far it travels afterward. Or use a spring scale if you already own one.

One trial can be affected by bumps, release mistakes, or measurement error. Repeat trials and look for a pattern. Say “In our test, felt produced shorter slide distances” rather than claiming one small setup proves a universal rule.

Words you will use

  • friction: contact force resisting relative sliding
  • controlled variable: a condition kept the same
  • trial: one repetition of an experiment
  • evidence: measurements supporting a claim
  • claim: a conclusion answering the investigation question

Worked examples

Fair test: same block, same ramp height, same start line, different surface covering.
Evidence: cardboard 82,79,81 cm vs towel 31,35,33 cm shows a repeated difference.
Overclaim: distance is an outcome affected by the setup; it is not a direct force measurement in newtons.

More worked examples

Teaching example: Which direction does friction act relative to sliding?
Answer / reasoning: Opposite the sliding or attempted sliding.
Teaching example: Why repeat trials?
Answer / reasoning: To reveal a pattern and reduce influence of one unusual trial.
Teaching example: Can you change surface and ramp height together and identify one cause?
Answer / reasoning: No.
