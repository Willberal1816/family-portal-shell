Ice-Melt Records: Compare the Measured Result

Emma · Science · Wednesday, October 28, 2026 · 40 minutes

What you are learning: Calculate measured melting, graph the actual change, and distinguish a supported comparison from a claim the record cannot prove.

Learn First

Change = starting mass - ending mass. Graph the amount melted, not the ending mass.

A fair comparison keeps cube size, time, and room conditions the same. One trial supports a result for that trial, not a universal claim.

Fully worked model

A cube starts at 30 g and ends at 21 g. Melted mass = 30 - 21 = 9 g. Write: “In this 12-minute trial, 9 g melted.”

Guided practice: reading measurements and drawing the graph

Model 2: A sample weighs 20 g before a trial and 16 g after, so 4 g melted. A second sample starts at 20 g and finishes at 13 g, so 7 g melted. For the two-bar graph, give the vertical scale grams melted, begin at zero, put the conditions along the bottom, and draw two bars reaching exactly 4 and 7. Model 3: “The second sample melted 3 g more in this trial” is supported. “This material always melts ice faster” is not supported by two readings.

Common mistake: Using the remaining mass (16 and 13) as a bar height instead of melted mass (4 and 7), or treating one trial as a universal law. The worksheet provides a blank labeled graph with tick marks so you can use your own calculated values. Controls make the comparison fair; repeating the same test makes evidence stronger.

Try it before printing: A cube goes from 25 g to 19 g. Calculate the melt loss and explain why the final mass is not the bar height.
