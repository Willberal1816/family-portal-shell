A Town Timeline: What Can We Prove?

Emma · Social Studies · Tuesday, September 29, 2026 · Learn First

What you are learning: connect dated historical sources to a careful claim without confusing what came first with what caused it. Read the examples, then use the separate Independent Work printable for all answers. This teaching page has no written work to turn in.

Learn the idea

A primary source was created around the event by someone recording or seeing it; a later source may use earlier evidence but represents a different date and perspective. A map records features as drawn for its time. A diary tells the writer’s experience; it does not speak for every person.

A historical explanation needs an event chain, a specific detail from each useful source, and a limit. Saying rain occurred before a delay may suggest a connection if the source reports it, but sequence alone cannot prove it was the only cause. Use exact dates and verbs, and distinguish a plausible explanation from a confirmed fact.

Words that help

  • primary source: a record created near the event
  • corroborate: compare independent evidence to see where it agrees
  • cause: a factor that actually helps produce an effect, not just an earlier event

Worked example 1

Worked example: A 1902 ledger says “the bridge closed Monday.” A 1902 letter says “my delivery arrived Tuesday.” These agree a closure and a delay occurred, but they do not prove all deliveries were late. Write the narrow claim and its source limit.

Worked example 2

A 1950 map cannot by itself establish exactly which path was used in 1902. First identify each document’s date and job. If two sources disagree, report the disagreement instead of silently choosing the dramatic one.
