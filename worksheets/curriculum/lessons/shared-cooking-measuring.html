Kitchen Measuring Lab

Emma + Natalie · Cooking / Life Skills · Learn First
What you are learning: Measure liquid and dry ingredients accurately, use common kitchen equivalents, and explain why technique affects a recipe.

Learn the idea

Cooking measurements are small experiments. If the amount changes greatly from one attempt to the next, a recipe can turn out differently. Accuracy starts with the right tool and a consistent technique.

For liquids, place a liquid measuring cup on a flat surface and bring your eyes level with the measurement line. For small quantities, measuring spoons are usually easier and more accurate.

For many dry ingredients, spoon the ingredient into a dry measuring cup and level the top. Do not pack an ingredient unless the recipe says to. Brown sugar is a common exception because recipes often specify packed brown sugar.

Useful equivalents let you scale recipes: 3 teaspoons = 1 tablespoon; 16 tablespoons = 1 cup; 1/2 cup = 8 tablespoons; 1/4 cup = 4 tablespoons. Equivalent fractions are real kitchen tools, not only paper math.

Words you will use

  • level: even with the top edge of a measuring cup or spoon
  • equivalent measure: a different measurement representing the same amount
  • liquid measure: a cup read while sitting on a flat surface
  • dry measure: a cup filled to the top and leveled

Worked examples

Example: 3/4 cup can be measured as 1/2 cup + 1/4 cup.
Example: 2 tablespoons = 6 teaspoons.
Technique: holding a liquid cup in the air can make the measurement line appear higher or lower because of viewing angle.

Guided checks

How can you make 3/4 cup with 1/2 and 1/4 cup measures?
One 1/2 cup plus one 1/4 cup.
How many teaspoons are in 2 tablespoons?
6 teaspoons.
Why place a liquid cup on a flat surface?
So the level is stable and can be read at eye height.

Before you practice

Strong work shows: correct tool choice, careful leveling/reading, equivalent-measure reasoning, and cleanup. Water can be used so no food is wasted.
