Clause Test — Can It Stand Alone?

What you are learning

A clause has its own subject and verb. Today you will use one question to decide what kind it is: If I read this whole clause by itself, does the thought feel finished?

Use the finish test

Finished thought → independent clause (I). It can stand as a sentence by itself.

Waiting thought → dependent clause (D). It has a subject and verb, but a word such as because, when, although, if, while, after, before, unless, or until makes you wait for more information.

Do not decide from a joining word alone. Read the whole clause aloud in your head and ask whether the message is complete.

Worked model 1 — hear the unfinished thought

Because the dog heard thunder, it hid under the desk.

Because the dog heard thunder → “Because the dog heard thunder…” leaves you waiting for what happened? It is D.

it hid under the desk → the thought is finished. It is I.

Pattern: D + I. That makes a complex sentence.

Worked model 2 — two finished thoughts

The rain stopped, so the game continued.

The rain stopped is finished → I.

the game continued is finished → I.

Pattern: I + I. That makes a compound sentence.

Worked model 3 — same idea, different pattern

When the rain stopped, the game continued.

When the rain stopped leaves you waiting → D. the game continued is finished → I.

Pattern: D + I. This one is complex, even though it tells almost the same event as the compound example.

Worked model 4 — more than two clauses

Although the road was wet, Mom drove slowly, and Dad watched for signs.

Although the road was wet → D. Mom drove slowly → I. Dad watched for signs → I.

There are at least two independent clauses and at least one dependent clause, so the sentence is compound-complex.

A mistake to catch

“After dinner ended” has a subject (dinner) and verb (ended), but after turns it into a waiting thought. Subject + verb is not enough by itself.

Your order is: find the whole clauses → use the finish test → mark I/D → name the sentence type.
