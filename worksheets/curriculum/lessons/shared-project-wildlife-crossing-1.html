Wildlife Crossing: Design and Test

Emma and Natalie · Project · Friday, September 25, 2026 · Learn First

What you are learning: design a safe crossing between two habitats, test realistic movement routes, and improve a map from the evidence. Read these worked examples before opening the separate Independent Work page. There is no written work to complete on this teaching page.

What a wildlife corridor does

Animals need routes between food, water and shelter. A road can interrupt a habitat. A corridor is a connected passage such as an overpass or underpass that lets animals cross without entering road traffic. A crossing needs safe entrances, continuity and a route animals can follow.

Model the design problem

Imagine forest on the left, a road through the middle, and a pond on the right. Draw a clearly labeled underpass at the shortest safe point and connect it to forest cover and pond habitat. A route is useful only when it connects both sides. A pretty bridge that ends in the road is not a safe corridor.

Use an evidence-first test

Make 10 small paper animal markers. Try 10 movement routes from the forest to the pond. Record how many follow the safe crossing without touching the road and where blocked routes fail. Redesign ONE part, then repeat ten trials under the same rules. Compare first and second results; name the improvement and a limit (paper movement is a model, not actual animal behavior).

What to record

Your independent record already supplies the map zones, 10-trial tally table, redesign box and final claim/evidence/reasoning space. Work on the printed sheet; do not draw an extra blank graph or table. Materials: printed record, pencil, two colored pencils or markers, 10 small scrap-paper markers, optional ruler.
