Fix the Sentence

Emma · Language Arts · Learn First
What you are learning: Recognize complete sentences, fragments, and run-ons, then revise them so ideas are clear and correctly punctuated.

Learn the idea

A complete sentence needs a subject—who or what the sentence is about—and a predicate that tells what the subject does or is. “The dog barked.” works because the reader knows who and what happened.

A fragment looks like part of a sentence but does not express a complete thought. “Because the dog barked.” makes the reader wait for what happened because of the barking. Add the missing idea: “Because the dog barked, the baby woke up.”

A run-on pushes complete sentences together without correct joining. A comma alone cannot safely join two complete sentences; that error is a comma splice. Repair a run-on with a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a joining word such as and, but, or so.

Editing is about meaning, not just punctuation. Read aloud, find each complete thought, decide which ideas belong together, then choose punctuation that shows the relationship clearly.

Words you will use

  • subject: who or what the sentence is about
  • predicate: what the subject does or is
  • fragment: an incomplete sentence
  • run-on: complete thoughts joined incorrectly
  • comma splice: two complete sentences joined with only a comma

Worked examples

Fragment: “After we finished dinner.” → “After we finished dinner, we played cards.”
Run-on: “The rain stopped we went outside.” → “The rain stopped, so we went outside.”
Comma splice: “I found my book, it was under the couch.” → “I found my book. It was under the couch.”

More worked examples

Teaching example: Is “Running through the muddy yard.” complete?
Answer / reasoning: No. It does not tell who is running.
Teaching example: Give two fixes for “Mia practiced, she felt ready.”
Answer / reasoning: Use a period, or use comma + so.
Teaching example: Why choose a period instead of “and”?
Answer / reasoning: A period gives complete ideas more separation or emphasis.
