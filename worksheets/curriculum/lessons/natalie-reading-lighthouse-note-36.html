Learn First

Read your own book for 25 minutes before the new short story. An inference uses a DETAIL directly from the text plus a reason. To explain mood, choose the exact setting details requested. A good sequence includes three named ACTIONS in order; a full ending says what the visitors saw or learned.

Two worked examples

Worked example 1. If a note says 'Use the blue-labeled box', 'She chose the blue box because the note said so' is an inference with DIRECT evidence.
Worked example 2. Instead of 'she looked around and fixed it', list 'read the chart → tested the two signals → labeled the correct device.' The ending says what happened after those steps.
Common mistake to avoid: Do not skip the middle action. If the question asks about visitors' learning, end with that result rather than only saying the team finished.

Today's new evidence or reading

The Lighthouse Note

Wind rattled the museum windows, and gray rain blurred the harbor lights. Nia was preparing a model lighthouse for a Friday evening tour. A small display card had fallen behind the table, leaving two lamp buttons with no labels. One made the model flash slowly; the other made it flash in rapid pairs.

The guide had asked Nia to demonstrate the harbor's historical warning pattern, but she could not remember which button produced it. On the shelf she found a dated demonstration note: 'Warning: two quick flashes. Welcome: one slow flash.' Instead of selecting the brighter-looking button, Nia checked the note.

First, she read the two descriptions carefully. Next, she tested each button and counted the flashes. Then she placed a 'warning' label beside the button with two quick flashes. The guide checked her result against the note before the visitors arrived.

During the tour, Nia demonstrated the warning signal from the indoor model. Visitors learned that different flash patterns carried different messages and that a written record helped identify the correct one.
