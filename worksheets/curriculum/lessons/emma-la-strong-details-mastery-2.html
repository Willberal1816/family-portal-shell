Strong Details — Show the Reader

What you are learning

A strong detail helps the reader see, understand, or believe the main idea. Today you will use a three-part test so you know exactly which details belong.

The three-part detail test

1. Specific: Does the detail name an exact action, object, sound, fact, or observation?

2. Relevant: Does it support the main idea instead of wandering away?

3. Useful: Does it help the reader picture what happened or understand why the main idea is true?

Worked model 1 — turn vague into visible

Main idea: The puppy was nervous at the vet.

Vague: “The puppy acted weird.”

Stronger: “The puppy pressed against my leg and tucked its tail when the exam-room door opened.”

The stronger version passes the test: the actions are specific, they support nervousness, and the reader can picture them.

Worked model 2 — remove a detail that does not help

Main idea: The bike ride was difficult.

Steep hill → relevant. Loose chain → relevant. Strong wind → relevant. Purple helmet → usually not relevant.

The helmet color may be specific, but it does not explain why the ride was difficult. A detail can be specific and still not belong.

Worked model 3 — choose an exact verb

Weak: “The squirrel went across the fence.”

Stronger: “The squirrel scrambled across the fence.”

Scrambled tells the reader how the movement looked. The sentence is clearer without adding extra words.

Worked model 4 — support a paragraph idea

Main idea: The library is a good place to concentrate.

Useful details could include: phones are kept quiet, study tables are separated from the entrance, and the room has few interruptions.

Each detail gives evidence for the main idea. A sentence about the color of the carpet would probably not help.

Before you revise

Ask: What is my main idea? Which exact detail proves or shows it? Can I replace a vague word with a precise noun or verb? You do not need longer sentences. You need clearer ones.
