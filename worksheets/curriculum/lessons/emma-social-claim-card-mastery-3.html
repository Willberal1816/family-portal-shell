Evidence Proof — Sort the Claim

What you are learning: Decide whether a source directly proves a claim, only suggests part of it, or does not support it at all.

You can find facts in sources. The part that still needs practice is stopping before adding a detail the source never gave. Today we will use claim cards instead of a proof ladder.

1. Three boxes for every claim

DIRECTLY PROVES

The source clearly states or shows the claim.

SUGGESTS / PARTLY SUPPORTS

The source supports part of the idea, but an important detail is still unknown.

NOT SUPPORTED

The source does not give the fact, cause, time, person, or detail claimed.

2. Worked source set

Source A — city alert, 7:40 a.m. “River Road is closed at the bridge because water is covering the roadway.”
Source B — photo caption, 8:05 a.m. “Water reaches the bottom of the bridge railing. Two orange barricades block the road.”
Source C — resident journal “Rain pounded the roof most of the night. I heard trucks near the bridge after sunrise.”

Claim: River Road was closed at the bridge. → Directly proved by Source A.

Claim: Water was high near the bridge. → Directly proved by A and B.

Claim: Heavy rain caused the bridge to collapse. → Not supported. C mentions rain, but none of the sources says the bridge collapsed or that rain caused structural damage.

Claim: Workers were repairing the bridge at sunrise. → Suggests at most. C heard trucks; it does not identify who was in them or what they were doing.

3. Name the missing fact

If a claim is not fully proved, say exactly what is missing. Example: “The source shows trucks nearby, but it does not identify the workers or say repairs were happening.”

This is stronger than writing “we don't know” because it explains the limit.

4. Causes need special evidence

Seeing Event A before Event B does not automatically prove A caused B. To claim cause, look for a source that actually connects them: an official report, a direct observation of the mechanism, a reliable statement from someone with knowledge, or another source that explains the link.

5. Build a safe conclusion

A strong conclusion combines only what the sources support: “Sources A and B show that high water covered River Road and the road was closed at the bridge. Source C reports heavy rain overnight, but the current sources do not prove why the water rose or whether the bridge was damaged.”

Independent-work routine: For each claim, mark Directly Proves / Suggests / Not Supported → name the source → copy or paraphrase the exact supporting detail → state what is still unknown.
