Kinds of Sentences — Build a Clause Blueprint

What you are learning: Build simple, compound, and complex sentences by first deciding which clauses can stand alone. Once the clause blueprint is clear, the sentence type and punctuation become much easier to see.

How this lesson works

Learn First: clause cards, the stand-alone test, blueprints for simple/compound/complex sentences, a punctuation decision path, and worked examples. Independent Work: fresh clause-marking, repair, classification, and construction tasks.

1. First decide whether each clause can stand alone

IC = independent clause has a subject and verb and can stand alone as a complete thought.

DC = dependent clause has a subject and verb but begins in a way that makes it depend on another thought.

Example:

Because the rain started+we went inside

“Because the rain started” has a subject and verb, but it leaves the reader waiting. It is DC.

2. Use blueprints instead of guessing from joining words

Simple: IC

Compound: IC+IC

Complex: DC+IC or IC+DC

Count complete clauses, not verbs or joining words.

3. A compound predicate is still one clause

The dog barked and scratched the door.

Subject = the dog. Verbs = barked and scratched. There is only one subject-verb thought that can stand alone, so the blueprint is IC. It is a simple sentence.

Compare: The dog barked, and the cat ran. Now each side has its own subject and verb and can stand alone: IC + IC. That is compound.

4. Punctuation follows the blueprint

BlueprintCommon punctuation
IC + IC with and/but/or/so/yet/for/norcomma before the joining word
DC first + ICcomma after the opening DC
IC + DCoften no comma when the DC is essential and comes after
IC IC with no connectorrun-on: split or join correctly

5. Worked blueprints

While the soup simmered, I washed the dishes.
While the soup simmered + I washed the dishes → complex.

I wanted to finish, but my eyes were tired.
I wanted to finish + my eyes were tired → compound.

My brother opened the box and checked the parts.
My brother opened the box and checked the parts → simple with a compound predicate.

6. Repair by identifying what you have

Run-on: “The timer rang we removed the muffins.” Blueprint: IC + IC but no legal join. Repair: “The timer rang, so we removed the muffins.”

Opening DC: “After the game ended we packed the chairs.” Blueprint: DC + IC. Repair: “After the game ended, we packed the chairs.”

Comma splice: “I called her, she answered.” Blueprint: IC + IC joined by only a comma. Repair with a period, semicolon, or comma + coordinating conjunction.

7. Traps to avoid

Trap: seeing and and automatically saying compound. Check whether there is an independent clause on both sides.

Trap: marking only one word when asked for a clause. A clause mark must include the whole subject-and-verb unit.

Trap: calling a sentence complex because it is long. Length does not decide the type; the clause blueprint does.

8. Independent mastery evidence

The printable uses new sentences and new contexts. Bracket each whole clause first, label IC/DC, then classify or punctuate. The clause blueprint—not a memorized joining word—is the evidence we need.
