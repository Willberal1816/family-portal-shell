A Better Library Schedule: Persuade With Limits

Natalie · Language Arts · Monday, November 16, 2026 · 55 minutes

What you are learning

Write an evidence-based persuasive letter that states a proposal, distinguishes known facts from assumptions, and addresses a counterargument.

Learn First

  1. A persuasive letter identifies its reader, a specific proposal and evidence. It must not change the source facts to make the argument stronger.
  2. The factual notice says library study room hours are 1-4 p.m. on Saturday and 2-5 p.m. on Sunday. The source does not say HOW MANY people use the room.
  3. A proposal asks for a change, such as keeping the room open until 5 on Saturday. A proposal is NOT a fact about what currently happens.
  4. A reason explains WHY a change might help. An evidence-based reason uses the notice; a personal guess must be named as a possibility.
  5. A counterargument describes a reasonable concern such as staffing. A fair reply acknowledges the uncertainty and suggests what evidence to collect.
  6. A strong claim cannot say 'everyone wants later hours' without a survey. Replace universal wording with a bounded plan or a question to investigate.
  7. Organize the finished letter with a greeting, position, two accurate facts, a counterpoint, a measured response and a clear request.

Two worked models

Model 1: Model A: Strong: 'The posted Saturday closing is 4 p.m. I recommend testing a 5 p.m. closing for one month and recording attendance.' Unsupported: 'All students need a 5 p.m. closing.'
Model 2: Model B: Counterargument: 'The library may need staffing.' Response: 'Before changing hours, the committee could check worker availability and track actual room use.' Neither is presented as a known fact.
A mistake to avoid: A proposed change is not an existing rule. Do not make up attendance numbers or claim universal approval.
