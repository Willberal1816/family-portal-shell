Check Another Source

Social Studies · Learn First
Goal: Learn how historians check one account against other evidence before deciding what probably happened.
School word: corroboration means checking one source against other independent evidence.

1. Why one source is not enough

A source can be honest and still be incomplete. One person may see only part of an event, remember details differently, or have a reason to focus on one side of the story.

2. A useful second source should be independent

If Source B simply copied Source A, it is not really a second piece of evidence. Better checks come from a different witness, newspaper, photograph, map, official record, object, or other source created separately.

3. Ask three questions when sources meet

  • Agree: What facts appear in more than one source?
  • Disagree: Where do the accounts conflict?
  • Add: What new information does one source contribute?

4. Practice case

The following sources are fictional practice sources.
Source A — diary: “The rain had been falling since before breakfast. By ten o'clock, water covered the lower end of Mill Street.”
Source B — town report: “At 10:20 a.m., workers closed Mill Street after water reached the bridge entrance.”
Source C — newspaper: “Residents said the lower road flooded during the morning storm, and traffic was turned away before noon.”

What agrees? All three place the flooding in the morning and connect it to Mill Street. What differs? The exact timing and what each source noticed. What does each add? The diary gives a witness view, the town report gives an official action and time, and the newspaper summarizes several reports.

5. Which source is “best”?

There is rarely one best source for every question. The town report may be strongest for when the road officially closed. The diary may be better for what one resident personally saw. Historians ask: best for which question?

6. Guided check

Teaching example: If two independent sources agree, does that prove they are correct?
Answer / reasoning: No. Agreement makes a claim stronger, but historians still consider how the sources got their information and whether other evidence exists.
Teaching example: If two honest witnesses disagree, must one be lying?
Answer / reasoning: No. They may have stood in different places, noticed different details, or remembered the event differently.
Teaching example: Why is rereading Source A not corroboration?
Answer / reasoning: Because it does not add independent evidence. You are checking the same source against itself.
Ready for practice when: you can explain “agree, disagree, add” and why independence matters.
