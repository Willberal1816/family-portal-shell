Reading: Who Is Speaking, and What Was Said?

Natalie | Reading | Friday, November 27, 2026 | 45 minutes

What you are learning: Read independently and distinguish literal speech, speaker attribution and narrator wording.

Learn First

Dialogue is the exact spoken wording in a story. Quotation marks often show the words a character speaks; speaker tags tell who said them. The narrator reports other events or descriptions. Do not confuse the narrator’s explanation with a character’s spoken statement.

A speaker can make a claim without the narrator proving it. Record the exact words and speaker, not a guessed feeling or motive. For nonfiction, use two exact statements attributed to named people or two clearly identified quotations.

Read your current book for 25 minutes. Locate a passage that includes speech or attributed statements. Use short exact phrases and real book page numbers. If your pages have no dialogue, identify two exact narrator statements and label them as narrator statements instead.

Two worked examples

Model p. 14: "I left the key on the shelf," Mira said. The narrator added that the shelf was empty. Speaker = Mira; spoken claim = left key on shelf; narrator observation = shelf empty. The text does not say why.
Model p. 15: "It might be in the bag," Arun said. Exact quote contains might, which expresses possibility. Replacing might with is changes the certainty. Verify the exact word and page rather than assume.

Independent work

Question 2. Copy a short exact spoken phrase, name its speaker and real page. If you have no dialogue, copy a narrator phrase and label it narrator.
Question 3. Choose a different phrase. Name its speaker or narrator and exact page. Explain the literal meaning in one sentence without guessing the motive.
