Clause Test — Can It Stand Alone?

For questions 1–5: Put brackets around every whole clause. Label each clause I or D. Under each D, write 2–5 words telling what makes the thought feel unfinished. Then name the sentence type.
1. The porch light came on, and the moths gathered near it.
2. While the cookies cooled, we washed the mixing bowls.
3. I packed a jacket because the forecast showed rain.
4. Although the trail was steep, we kept climbing, and Maya carried the water.
5. Dad opened the windows, but we closed them when the storm arrived.

6. Correct the decision. A student says Because the alarm rang is independent because it has a subject and a verb. Write the correct label and then write 2 sentences explaining why subject + verb is not enough.
7. Compare the patterns.
A. The movie ended, so we turned on the lights.
B. When the movie ended, we turned on the lights.
For each sentence, bracket and label every clause and name the sentence type. Then write 2 sentences explaining exactly why so and when lead to different I/D patterns.
8. Repair a fragment. Start with the dependent clause Although the bus was late. Add one independent clause to make a complete complex sentence. Bracket both clauses and label them D and I.

9. Build a compound sentence. Write a sentence with exactly two independent clauses joined correctly. Bracket both clauses, label I + I, and underline the joining word or punctuation.
10. Build a compound-complex sentence. Write one sentence with exactly two independent clauses and one dependent clause. Bracket all three clauses, label each I or D, and use punctuation that matches the structure.
11. Explain the finish test. In 3–4 complete sentences, explain how you decide whether a clause is independent or dependent and how the I/D pattern tells you whether a sentence is compound, complex, or compound-complex.
